# Introducing Stonefish v4.5

Stonefish v4.5 is the moment the Stonefish revolution gains something it has never had before: **knowledge before calculation**.

Stonefish v4 already knew how to calculate tactically and separate equal moves with positional judgment. Stonefish v4.5 keeps that entire foundation, then adds three new knowledge layers on top:

1. a weighted 50-line opening repertoire,
2. named mating-pattern recognition and conversion,
3. inverse positional logic that evaluates how effectively a move restricts the opponent.

The result is an engine that does not merely react well once a position appears. It can now enter the game with a plan, recognize familiar winning geometry, and actively reduce the opponent’s freedom.

## The headline feature: a real opening repertoire

The biggest change in Stonefish v4.5 is the opening book.

Previous Stonefish models had to build the opening from general principles and tactical rules. v4 could prefer development, castling, mobility, king safety, center control, and disciplined queen placement — but every opening still had to emerge from those generic rules one move at a time.

Stonefish v4.5 changes that completely.

It ships with a **50-line weighted opening repertoire: 25 lines for White and 25 for Black**. The repertoire is deliberately concentrated around high-theory, engine-friendly openings and main lines rather than treating every possible opening equally.

For White, the repertoire includes systems such as the Ruy Lopez Berlin and Closed lines, Italian Pianissimo, Scotch, Petroff, Sicilian Najdorf English Attack, Sveshnikov, Rossolimo, Caro-Kann Advance, French Tarrasch, Open and Closed Catalans, QGD Exchange, Semi-Slav Meran, Nimzo-Indian Classical, Gruenfeld Exchange, King’s Indian Bayonet, Queen’s Gambit Accepted, Slav, Queen’s Indian, English systems, Reti, Trompowsky, and Vienna Gambit.

For Black, Stonefish can enter the Berlin and Petroff, multiple Sicilian families including the Najdorf, Sveshnikov, Taimanov and Accelerated Dragon, the Caro-Kann, French structures, Scandinavian, QGD, Nimzo-Indian, Queen’s Indian, Semi-Slav, Slav, Gruenfeld, King’s Indian, Queen’s Gambit Accepted, Dutch Leningrad, Benko, Bogo-Indian, English responses, Reti structures, and Pirc.

This is not just variety for the sake of variety. Each line carries a repertoire weight, and the White and Black repertoires are each normalized around deliberate preferences. Mainline, engine-tested structures receive more emphasis; rarer systems remain available without dominating selection.

## Weighted, not deterministic

Stonefish v4.5 does not simply pick one favorite opening and repeat it forever.

When multiple compatible book lines are available, the engine chooses among them using weighted selection. A line with a higher repertoire prior is more likely to be selected, but lower-weight lines remain live.

That preserves one of Stonefish’s oldest traits — variation — while replacing opening randomness with informed variation.

The engine can therefore favor highly trusted structures without becoming trivially predictable.

## The book follows the real game

The most important part of the opening system is what happens when the opponent does not cooperate.

A weak opening book can become brittle: it knows one sequence, the opponent deviates, and the book is suddenly useless.

Stonefish v4.5 does something smarter.

It tracks the actual move history and continuously checks which repertoire lines are still compatible with the position reached on the board. If the selected line remains valid, Stonefish continues following it.

If the opponent leaves that branch, Stonefish **drops the broken line and re-weights only the remaining opening lines that still match the real game**.

That means the book does not merely memorize one path. It behaves like a live repertoire tree.

A chosen line can fail without the entire opening system failing. Stonefish simply asks: among the prepared structures that are still possible, which one should I follow now?

That compatible-line fallback is the core of the v4.5 opening architecture.

## Knowledge without blind obedience

The book is powerful, but it is not absolute.

Stonefish v4.5 checks for immediate mating opportunities and strong mating-pattern takeovers **before** obeying the opening book. If the board presents a forced tactical finish, Stonefish does not continue reciting theory while checkmate is available.

Once no strong mating pattern overrides the position, the book gets priority. When no compatible book move remains, Stonefish falls back to the tactical and positional engine beneath it.

The hierarchy is deliberate:

- finish the game if a convincing mating opportunity is already present,
- otherwise use prepared opening knowledge while the real game still matches the repertoire,
- otherwise return to Stonefish’s tactical and positional decision system.

So v4.5 gains theory without losing tactical awareness.

## Named mating-pattern recognition

The second major knowledge layer is mating geometry.

Stonefish v4.5 does not only recognize literal mate-in-one. It scores positions for recognizable mating structures and can take over with a pattern move when the geometry becomes strong enough.

The engine contains explicit recognition for **ladder mates, triangle formations, back-rank nets, smothered-mate geometry, Arabian mate structures, queen-and-rook kill boxes, and Boden-style bishop patterns**.

It also measures the enemy king’s freedom and rewards moves that tighten a mating net, especially when the king is trapped near the edge of the board or has very few safe squares remaining.

A fully available checkmate still outranks everything. But v4.5 can now recognize the shape of a finish before the final move appears.

## It starts asking what the opponent loses

Stonefish v4 introduced positional intelligence by asking whether Stonefish itself gains mobility, king safety, central control, board control, and other strategic advantages.

v4.5 adds the inverse question.

Among otherwise strong candidates, it can now prefer moves that:

- reduce the opponent’s legal mobility,
- reduce the number of checking replies available to the opponent,
- give check when appropriate,
- restrict the enemy king’s freedom,
- reduce the opponent’s center control,
- reduce the opponent’s total board control.

That is a subtle but important shift.

A good position is no longer defined only by what Stonefish can do. It is also defined by what the opponent can no longer do.

## Everything v4 already knew is still here

Stonefish v4.5 does not replace the v4 engine. It extends it.

The v3 tactical core still handles mate safety and three-ply material reasoning. The v4 positional chain still evaluates promotion strength, castling, opening development, hanging-piece safety, repetition control, mobility, queen discipline, fifty-move resets, endgame pawn progress, checks, piece support, king freedom, center control, minor-piece centralization, king protection, pawn structure, rook activity, king placement, and board control.

v4.5 then inserts opponent-restriction criteria beside those existing concepts and uses mating-pattern knowledge to separate otherwise equal moves.

So the upgrade is cumulative: calculation, position, repertoire knowledge, mating geometry, and restriction all coexist.

## The test: 2,000 direct games

For the v4.5 benchmark, only games involving Stonefish v4.5 matter: 1,000 games against Stonefish v3 and 1,000 games against Stonefish v4.

Across those 2,000 direct games, Stonefish v4.5 produced:

**1,333 wins · 174 losses · 493 draws**

That is a **66.6% win rate** with an **8.7% loss rate**.

### v4.5 vs v4: 760 wins, zero losses

This is the defining comparison.

Across 1,000 direct games:

- Stonefish v4.5: **760 wins, 0 losses, 240 draws**
- Stonefish v4: **0 wins, 760 losses, 240 draws**
- Stonefish v4.5 win rate: **76.0%**

Stonefish v4.5 did not lose a single game to v4.

That result matters because the two engines share so much underlying tactical and positional machinery. The difference is the new knowledge layer: prepared openings, mating-pattern recognition, and opponent restriction.

**Against v4, that knowledge layer turned a shared foundation into a 760–0 result.**

### v4.5 vs v3: 573–174

Against Stonefish v3, v4.5 won **573**, lost **174**, and drew **253** across 1,000 games.

That is a **57.3% win rate** against an engine already capable of three-ply worst-case calculation.

## Why does v3 fare better against v4.5 than v4 does?

At first glance, this looks backwards. Stonefish v4 beat Stonefish v3 convincingly, yet v3 takes 174 games from v4.5 while v4 takes none.

That is not a contradiction. Chess-engine strength is not perfectly transitive, and v4.5 is not merely “v4 but stronger by the same amount against everyone.” It changes the matchup itself.

Stonefish v4 and v4.5 share the same positional vocabulary. v4 tries to improve mobility, king freedom, center control, development, structure, activity, and board control. v4.5 keeps those ideas, then adds inverse versions that explicitly reduce the opponent’s mobility, checking chances, king freedom, center control, and board control.

That means v4.5 is unusually well-equipped to attack the exact qualities v4 is trying to optimize. The two engines are speaking the same positional language, but v4.5 can answer many of v4’s priorities with a direct restriction layer on top.

Stonefish v3 is different. It is simpler, more tactical, and less dependent on that positional tie-breaking stack. Its decisions are driven more directly by three-ply worst-case material calculation. Because of that, some of v4.5’s positional suppression machinery has less to latch onto. v3 can also produce less structured, less predictable positions that leave the prepared opening and positional layers doing more adaptation.

The test results therefore suggest a classic matchup effect: **v4 is the stronger engine against v3, but v3 is the less convenient opponent for v4.5.**

That does not mean v3 is stronger than v4. v4.5 still beats v3 clearly, 573–174. It means the path from one version to the next is not a perfectly straight ladder. Different decision systems create different weaknesses, and v4.5 happens to punish v4’s style exceptionally hard.

## Why the opening book matters so much

The opening book changes the game before the ordinary engine has to solve anything.

Instead of spending the first phase repeatedly rediscovering development, central tension, castling structures, and piece placement from generic heuristics, Stonefish can begin inside prepared, coherent systems.

That provides several advantages at once.

**It gives Stonefish structure immediately.** Pieces can arrive in coordinated positions because the engine is following complete opening ideas rather than independently selecting each developing move.

**It reduces early self-inflicted mistakes.** General heuristics can produce sensible moves, but a repertoire can encode move order and interaction between moves that isolated rules cannot capture as cleanly.

**It preserves diversity.** Weighted selection means Stonefish is not locked into a single opening personality.

**It survives deviations.** Compatible-line fallback means an opponent cannot break the entire book simply by stepping away from one chosen branch.

**It hands the middlegame engine better positions.** The tactical and positional layers begin their real work from structures chosen intentionally rather than accidentally.

The opening book is not just a faster way to choose the first few moves. It changes the quality of the positions the rest of Stonefish gets to play.

## From principles to preparation

Stonefish v1 was unpredictable.

Stonefish v2 became aware.

Stonefish v3 learned to calculate.

Stonefish v4 learned to build positions.

**Stonefish v4.5 arrives prepared.**

For the first time, Stonefish enters a game carrying a repertoire, recognizes named mating geometry, and measures not only its own possibilities but the opponent’s restrictions.

The engine has moved beyond asking only, “What is good here?”

It can now ask, **“What do we already know about this position — and how do we force the game into territory we understand?”**
