MODEL RELEASE · STONEFISH V5 PRO

Introducing Stonefish v5 Pro

Stonefish v5 Pro takes the unified points architecture and adds context, threat awareness, and deeper verification before committing.

Stonefish v5 changed the engine by putting every legal move on one scoreboard. Stonefish v5 Pro keeps that foundation, then adds adaptive weights, richer tactical geometry, counterplay suppression, confidence-aware scoring, and a selective five-ply alpha-beta search.

The result is not just stronger play. It is one of the most interesting benchmark patterns in the Stonefish project.

Adaptive points

Pro no longer treats every phase of chess the same way. It estimates opening, attack, defence, conversion, endgame, and pawn-race context, then changes the importance of different features accordingly.

Development matters more while the opening is alive. King safety becomes more important under pressure. King activity grows in the endgame. Passed pawns become more valuable when promotion races begin. When Stonefish is ahead, suppressing counterplay and converting cleanly receive extra weight.

The scorecard stays unified. The meaning of the score changes with the position.

Threat geometry becomes explicit

Stonefish v5 Pro measures loose pieces and coordination, attacker-versus-defender counts, king-zone pressure, pins and skewers, under-defended pieces, enemy checking chances, forcing moves, promotion threats, and dangerous passed pawns.

That lets it distinguish between positions that look similar on material and mobility but carry very different tactical risk.

Confidence-aware scoring

Pro also asks whether independent systems agree. A move gains confidence when tactical evaluation, adaptive positional evaluation, inherited Stonefish knowledge, and forcing characteristics point in the same direction.

If a position looks attractive strategically while tactics strongly disagree, Pro can actively distrust the pretty move.

Five-ply search

Stonefish v5 evaluates tactics through three plies. Pro adds exactly two more searched plies.

Every legal move first receives a preliminary points-based score. Pro then keeps the top eight candidates and searches those to five plies with alpha-beta pruning. The deeper result is blended back with the preliminary score, while mating results can override the blend outright.

This means a move that looks best after three plies can now be challenged by what happens two moves later.

Then the benchmark does something unexpected: v4.5 and Pro split the entire 1,000-game matchup exactly in half.

The triangle should not be possible if strength were a simple ladder

The three direct results are extreme:

v5 beats v4.5 1000–0. Pro beats v5 1000–0. Pro and v4.5 go 500–500.

That is a textbook reminder that chess-engine strength can be matchup-dependent. A model can dominate one opponent without inheriting the same margin against every engine that opponent previously beat.

Why might v4.5 be so much harder for Pro than v5?

The code suggests several plausible mechanisms.

They make decisions differently. v5 and Pro share the unified points architecture, so Pro's deeper and more adaptive evaluation attacks v5 on familiar ground. v4.5 is structurally different: it is much more book-driven and lexicographic, so it can reach positions that do not interact with Pro's improvements in the same way.

Pro is selective. Every legal move gets a preliminary score, but only the top eight are searched to five plies. That is normally a major strength. It also creates a distinct failure mode: if an awkward v4.5 position pushes the truly resilient move outside that preliminary shortlist, deeper search never gets the chance to recover it.

The opening repertoire can shape the entire matchup. v4.5 can force games through prepared branches and compatible-line fallback. Pro can deviate from book through evaluation, but its adaptive priorities are not identical to v5's fixed unified scoring, so it can choose very different paths from the model that beat v4.5 1000–0.

The exact 500–500 with zero draws looks systematic. A perfectly even split across 1,000 decisive games is consistent with a strong color, opening-branch, or deterministic interaction. Without per-color or per-opening logs, that cannot be claimed as the cause, but it is hard to read the result as ordinary random variance.

Pro is clearly stronger than v5 head-to-head. v4.5 simply asks a different question of it.

Deeper does not mean universally dominant

Stonefish v5 Pro is deeper, more adaptive, and tactically richer than v5. The 1000–0 result confirms how devastating that can be against the model it directly extends.

But the v4.5 split is just as valuable a result. It reveals that opening structure, candidate selection, and opponent style can reshape the value of an engine's improvements.

That makes Pro more interesting than a simple "v5 but stronger" release.

From judgment to adaptation

Stonefish v1 was unpredictable. Stonefish v2 became aware. Stonefish v3 learned to calculate. Stonefish v4 learned to build. Stonefish v4.5 arrived prepared. Stonefish v5 learned to judge.

Stonefish v5 Pro learns to adapt — and then looks deeper before it commits.

Strength is not one number. Pro proves it can dominate one matchup while another opponent exposes an entirely different game.

MODEL RELEASE · STONEFISH V5

Introducing Stonefish v5

Stonefish v5 rewrites the decision system around one idea: every legal move gets a complete score before Stonefish decides.

Earlier Stonefish models became stronger by adding new layers: tactical calculation, positional judgment, opening knowledge, mating patterns, and opponent restriction. Stonefish v5 changes how all of those ideas interact.

Two upgrades define the release: a unified points architecture, and an opening book that has become advice instead of law.

Change one: every move gets points

Every legal root move stays eligible. Every one receives tactical, positional, strategic, and knowledge points. The move with the strongest total score wins.

Stonefish v5 scores material, mobility, development, center control, minor-piece centralization, king protection, king freedom, pawn structure, rook activity, king placement, board control, hanging pieces, bishop-pair value, passed pawns, repetition pressure, mating knowledge, and opponent restriction.

Stonefish no longer asks which move survives the hierarchy. It asks which move has the strongest complete case.

Change two: the opening book becomes advice

Stonefish v4.5 introduced the weighted opening repertoire. v5 keeps that preparation, but the book move now receives a strong point bonus instead of automatically controlling the move.

If theory and the full evaluation agree, Stonefish follows the book. If another move earns a higher total, Stonefish can deliberately reject theory.

The book can win the argument. It no longer automatically wins the vote.

MODEL RELEASE · STONEFISH V4.5

Introducing Stonefish v4.5

Stonefish v4.5 adds knowledge before calculation: opening preparation, mating-pattern recognition, and opponent restriction.

A 50-line opening repertoire

Stonefish v4.5 includes a weighted 50-line opening repertoire: 25 lines for White and 25 for Black, centered on high-theory, engine-friendly main lines.

If the opponent leaves the selected branch, v4.5 discards it and re-weights only the repertoire lines still compatible with the actual game.
